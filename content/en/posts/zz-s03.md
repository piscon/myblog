## I. Uncannily Punctual

I discovered it on my third day of moving in: **every day at exactly six o'clock **, the sound of a piano would drift in from next door. It wasn't a full piece, just a segment of practice lasting about two minutes—mostly scales, interspersed with a few repeatedly tested chords, before stopping at the exact same spot.

I made a point of recording it for a week:

| Day 期 | Start 始 | Stop at 哪 | Afterward |
|---|---|---|---|
| 一 | 18:00 | Third bar 节 | Quiet |
| 三 | 18:00 | Third bar 节 | Quiet |
| 五 | 17:58 | Third bar 节 | One extra attempt |
| 日 | 18:01 | Second bar 节 | Quiet |

- [x] Recorded for seven full days

- [ ] Never went to knock on the door

> It was so punctual it made me wonder if the person playing was also waiting for this specific mark in the day.

## II. That Little Segment Where It Stops

What I care about most is actually the "stop." The first half of the two minutes is very smooth, but by the third bar, it will **slow down **, try twice, and then it's gone. It isn't a panicked break caused by a mistake; it is **a very calm cessation of playing **.

One day, I happened to be washing a cup in the kitchen. The sound of the water muffled half of it, and by the time I turned off the tap, it had already gone quiet over there. The cup in my hand was still dripping; I stood there dazed for a few seconds, not exactly feeling disappointed, but more like **I had missed out on an unfinished sentence **.

*Later, I developed a habit: turning the water down a bit before six o'clock.* It was purely meddling, but it didn't hurt anything.

## III. Regarding "Whether to Knock"

I've thought many times about whether I should go and ask. My reasons are neatly lined up:

1. Ask for the name of the piece, and thank them while I'm at it
2. Say "that part sounds lovely"
3. ~~What should I send over? ~~ (It's too heavy)
4. Say nothing, just keep listening.

Option 4 was the ultimate winner. It wasn't out of shyness, but because I felt that once ** asked, those two minutes would turn into "who is this being played for" **. This way is just fine: the player doesn't need to know someone is next door, and the listener doesn't need to acknowledge they heard it.

> Some distance isn't estrangement; it's space left for the sound itself.

A clerk at the convenience store downstairs once chatted with me, saying, "That person practicing at six, right? I've been listening from here for two years." I nodded, not mentioning that I've also been keeping track for a week. **Knowing that I'm not the only one ** quietly listening made me quite happy.

## Four: In a Rain That Never Fell

Last Thursday, the weather was unusually stifling. By six o'clock, the sky had already darkened to a deep blue, and the piano music began as usual. At the third bar, a distant roll of thunder happened to rumble outside the window—not loud, just like someone gently nudging a table.

The piano stopped for about three seconds, and then ** played the third bar from the beginning again **; this time it flowed through, connecting to the fourth bar, played four notes, and then stopped.

I didn't hear anything after the fourth bar later that day. But I suppose I finished listening to half a piece.

**It wasn't that the piece was finally finished, but that on that day, it was allowed to move forward just a little bit. **

## Five: Six O'Clock This Morning

I got off work early today and arrived home at five to five. I placed my keys into the cracked ceramic bowl in the entryway, didn't turn on the tap, didn't turn on the light, and just sat by the window waiting.

At six sharp, the scales began. The wind drifted in through the gap in the window that was never quite closed, carrying a hint of the sycamore leaves from downstairs.

I didn't go knock on that door. I probably won't in the future, either.

> A sound that appears punctually every day, yet demands no response, was the first thing that made me feel "I can stay here" after moving into this new place.
