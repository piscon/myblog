##  I. Returned Mail

It came back nine days after I sent it out. There was a stamp on the envelope so faint it was almost illegible, with a line of small handwriting next to it: "Insufficient Postage." The bottom right corner, where I had originally written the address, was worn fuzzy, and the corner of the paper was curled, as if someone had repeatedly fiddled with it along the way.

>  It didn't reach its destination; instead, ** it took a round trip on my behalf **.

The front of the postcard showed a river embankment at dusk under an orange sky. On the back, I had written three lines:

1.  The plane trees here are starting to shed their leaves
2.  From the windowsill of my new home, I can see the lights of the building opposite
3.  "Write back once you've settled in, no rush"

The English in the third line was copied from a note on a tea bag a friend had tucked into my things. The handwriting was ugly, but at the time, I felt it was ** exactly what I wanted to say **.

##  II. Miscalculated Postage

The problem was that I calculated based on the postcard alone, forgetting that I had tucked it inside a ** thin kraft paper envelope **. Those extra few grams meant it was short by one stamp.

##  III. Why was nothing resent?

The returned item sat on my desk for two days. Adding a stamp and dropping it back into that green mailbox downstairs took less than five minutes. But the more I stared at its frayed edges, the more I felt that ** it was no longer the original one **.

The original was "words I sent to a friend." This returned one had extra postmarks, extra handwriting from others, and an extra journey that I didn't take. ** Sending it out again, what I’d be sending was this round trip, not those three lines of text. **

>  Some things, once they return carrying an experience, are no longer suitable to be treated as if nothing ever happened.

I sent my friend a message, typing out the three lines exactly as they were, adding a sentence at the end: "I originally sent a piece of paper, but it decided to go on a little trip and came back on its own." She replied with a string of laughter, saying "** that paper traveled before I did **," and then we chatted about other things for half an hour.

##  Four, later it went into a drawer

I didn't throw it away, nor did I resend it. I tucked it into that unfinished book by the entryway, right on top of a whole sycamore leaf I had picked up during a walk.

Later, I received things from my friend: a packet of tea, a photo of a riverbank from her side, and a note that read, "Enough stamps this time." ** She clearly treated this as a joke that could continue **, which was nice.

I tucked the note into the same page. Now, that book holds a leaf, a returned postcard, and a note with enough stamps; none of them are weighing down the others.

##  Five, a somewhat useless conclusion

Thinking about it, what the lost mailing taught me wasn't "make sure to use enough stamps"—you can just look that up. It was that **"delivery" is not the only thing that can happen to a letter.**

-  It might walk a little extra distance for you
-  It might bring back someone else's handwriting
-  It might make a phrase like "no rush" truly become no rush

There is no wind outside tonight. That green mailbox downstairs still stands in its place, its slot wiped quite clean. As I passed by, I patted my pocket; I had nothing to mail, and I didn't feel like I was missing anything.

>  The one that wasn't delivered didn't end up being lost, after all. It just found a different place to stay.
